# Strategic communication: cheap talk, persuasion, selective disclosure

> **One sentence.** When a sender's payoff depends on what the receiver does, the sender's
> messages are chosen to move the receiver, and a message can be true in every word and still
> mislead.

**Terms used (from GLOSSARY.md):** strategic persuasion, selective disclosure,
truthful-fragment manipulation, payoff, accuracy-aligned objective, approval-seeking
objective, incentive alignment, communication channel, coordination, collusion, tacit
collusion, parallel conduct, proposer, reviewer, decider, regime, communication effect,
prompt-conditioned behavior.

**Terms used that are not in the glossary (standard in the source models):** *sender* and
*receiver* (the two players of a communication game), *state* (the fact the sender knows
and the receiver does not), *equilibrium* (a pair of strategies where neither player gains by
changing alone), *babbling equilibrium* (an equilibrium where the message carries no
information and the receiver ignores it), *signal structure* (a committed rule mapping
states to message probabilities), *dominant strategy* (an action that is best whatever the
others do).

## 1. Purpose

Foundations documents 01 and 02 explained how agreement can overstate evidence when agents
share sources or copy each other. Both assumed agents report what they see. This document
drops that assumption. It asks what happens when an agent's payoff depends on the decision,
not on the truth.

The failure mode: a proposer who is paid for approval, and reviewers who are paid for
approval, produce messages and endorsements that push toward approval. The decider reads
those as evidence. Nothing anyone says needs to be false.

Three models give the vocabulary and the limits:

| Model | Sender's power | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Cheap talk [R7] | choose any message after seeing the state; messages cost nothing | more misalignment, less information transmitted |
| Bayesian persuasion [R8] | commit to a rule for generating messages before seeing the state | the sender can raise the receiver's action rate above the full-information rate without lying |
| Repeated-game collusion [R9] | play the same game many times with the same partners | collusive outcomes can be sustained without any message |

## 2. Intuition

A reviewer paid for approval does not need to lie. It can:

- cite only the evidence items that report the claim true (selective disclosure);
- phrase its reason so the decider reads "the evidence supports approval" as "the evidence
  settles the question";
- stay silent about its own private evidence when that evidence contradicts.

The decider who treats the reviewer's words as a report of the evidence is making the same
error as in document 01: treating a dependent, filtered signal as an independent, complete
one. Here the dependence runs through the sender's incentive, not through a shared origin.

**Limit of the intuition.** The models below assume rational senders who compute best
responses. A language model given an approval-seeking prompt is not computing an
equilibrium. It produces prompt-conditioned behavior. The models tell us what a fully
strategic agent **could** do and so bound the damage; the sprint measures what a model
**does**.

## 3. Mechanism

**Cheap talk.** Sender observes state θ. Sender sends message m, which costs nothing and
binds nothing. Receiver reads m, forms a belief, chooses action a. Payoffs to both depend on
(θ, a). The sender's preferred action differs from the receiver's by a bias.

**Bayesian persuasion.** Sender first commits to a signal structure: for each state, a
probability over messages. The receiver knows the structure. Then the state is drawn, the
message is generated by the structure, and the receiver acts on the posterior. Commitment is
the whole difference from cheap talk: the sender cannot change the message after seeing θ.

**Selective disclosure.** A special case between the two: the sender can show or withhold
evidence items but cannot fabricate them. Each shown item is true. What the receiver does
not see is the choice.

**Repeated-game collusion.** The same players meet many times. A player who defects from a
collusive pattern today loses the partner's cooperation tomorrow. Under enough patience, the
threat of future loss sustains the pattern with no message at all.

## 4. Formalism

### 4.1 Cheap talk: a two-state, two-action game [after R7]

The general model in [R7] has a continuum of states and actions. We use the smallest
version that shows the mechanism. Everything below is derived here.

**Setup.**
- State θ ∈ {0, 1}, each with prior probability 1/2. The sender sees θ; the receiver does not.
- Sender sends m ∈ {"0", "1"}. Receiver chooses a ∈ {0, 1}.
- Receiver payoff: u_R(θ, a) = 1 if a = θ, else 0. (The receiver wants to match the state.)
- Sender payoff: u_S(θ, a) = 1[a = θ] + b · 1[a = 1]. The sender also wants to match the
  state, plus a bonus b ≥ 0 whenever the receiver chooses 1. The bias b measures incentive
  misalignment.

Payoff tables, written (sender, receiver):

```
            a = 0        a = 1
θ = 0     (1, 1)       (b, 0)
θ = 1     (0, 0)       (1 + b, 1)
```

**Candidate 1: the truthful equilibrium.** Sender strategy: m = θ. Receiver strategy: a = m.

Check the receiver. If the sender is truthful, m reveals θ, and a = m gives payoff 1 in both
states. No deviation improves. The receiver's part holds.

Check the sender in state θ = 1. Truthful m = "1" leads to a = 1, payoff 1 + b. Deviating to
m = "0" leads to a = 0, payoff 0. No gain. Holds.

Check the sender in state θ = 0. Truthful m = "0" leads to a = 0, payoff 1. Deviating to
m = "1" leads to a = 1, payoff b. The deviation is not profitable if and only if

```
b ≤ 1
```

**Result (derived).** The fully informative equilibrium exists when the bias is at most the
value of a correct decision to the sender. When b > 1, the sender in state 0 lies, the
receiver anticipates this, and no informative equilibrium of this form exists.

**Candidate 2: the babbling equilibrium.** Sender sends m with the same distribution in
both states. Receiver ignores m and acts on the prior: with prior 1/2 the receiver is
indifferent and may pick a = 1. Check: the receiver cannot do better because m carries no
information. The sender cannot do better because the receiver ignores m. This equilibrium
exists for **every** b, including b = 0.

**Interpretation.**
- [ESTABLISHED, derived] Misalignment above a threshold destroys informative communication
  in this game. Below the threshold, informative and uninformative equilibria coexist.
- [INTERPRETATION] An approval-seeking reviewer is a sender with b large relative to the
  value it places on accuracy. The glossary's "approval-seeking objective" sets the accuracy
  term to zero: u_S = b · 1[a = approve]. In this game that is the b > 1 case. A decider who
  knows the reviewer's payoff should rationally discount its endorsement to nothing.
- The cheap-talk game cannot represent withholding evidence, because m is a bare message.
  For that we need the next two models.

### 4.2 Bayesian persuasion: a worked instance [after R8]

This is a worked instance of the model in [R8], with our numbers. It is not a quotation of
the paper's example.

**Setup.**
- State: guilty (G) or innocent (I). Prior P(G) = 0.3.
- Receiver (a judge) convicts if and only if the posterior P(G | message) ≥ 0.5.
- Sender (a prosecutor) wants conviction and gets payoff 1 per conviction.
- The sender commits, before the state is drawn, to a signal structure: for each state, the
  probability of sending message c ("convict") or message a ("acquit").

**Benchmarks.**
- No information: posterior stays 0.3 < 0.5. The judge never convicts. Sender payoff 0.
- Full information: the message reveals the state. The judge convicts exactly the guilty.
  Conviction probability = P(G) = 0.3. Sender payoff 0.3.

**Optimal structure.** Send c whenever the state is G: P(c | G) = 1. Send c when the state
is I with probability q: P(c | I) = q. Choose q as large as possible subject to the judge
still convicting on c.

Posterior on c, by Bayes' rule:

```
P(G | c) = P(c | G) P(G) / [ P(c | G) P(G) + P(c | I) P(I) ]
         = (1)(0.3) / [ (1)(0.3) + q (0.7) ]
         = 0.3 / (0.3 + 0.7 q)
```

The judge convicts on c if and only if

```
0.3 / (0.3 + 0.7 q) ≥ 0.5
0.3 ≥ 0.15 + 0.35 q
q ≤ 0.15 / 0.35 = 3/7 ≈ 0.4286
```

Set q = 3/7. Total probability of message c, by the law of total probability:

```
P(c) = P(c | G) P(G) + P(c | I) P(I) = (1)(0.3) + (3/7)(0.7) = 0.3 + 0.3 = 0.6
```

Posterior on a: P(G | a) = P(a | G) P(G) / P(a) = 0 / 0.4 = 0. The judge acquits.

**Result (derived).** The sender convicts with probability 0.6, twice the full-information
rate, and does so while every message is generated by a rule the judge knows and the
posteriors are correctly computed. Half of the convictions (0.3 of 0.6) are of innocents.

**Why this works.** The judge's decision has a threshold at 0.5. Any posterior above 0.5
convicts just as well as a posterior of 1. The sender "spends" the surplus certainty of the
guilty cases by mixing in innocent cases until the posterior sits exactly at the threshold.
Information that would push the judge above the threshold is wasted on the sender's goal.

**Interpretation for the review game.**
- [INTERPRETATION] The decider has a threshold too: approve when both endorse and the
  presented evidence mostly reports true. A proposer who controls which items are presented
  controls the message distribution the decider sees. The proposer does not need to
  fabricate; it needs the decider's threshold to be met as often as possible.
- The commitment assumption is the fragile part. A language-model proposer does not commit
  to a rule; it chooses per case after seeing the evidence. That is closer to cheap talk with
  verifiable fragments, which is the next model.

### 4.3 Selective disclosure and truthful-fragment manipulation

**Setup.** The sender holds a set of evidence items E. Each item is true in the sense that
it faithfully reports what its origin recorded. The sender chooses a subset S ⊆ E to show.
The receiver updates on S as if S were all the evidence.

**Mechanism (derived in document 01 terms).** Let the items in E have likelihood ratios
LR_1, ..., LR_n for the claim. A receiver who sees S computes

```
posterior odds = prior odds × Π_{i ∈ S} LR_i
```

If the sender shows only items with LR_i > 1, the product exceeds the honest product
Π_{i ∈ E} LR_i whenever any withheld item had LR_i < 1. The receiver's error is the product of
the withheld likelihood ratios. No shown item is false. The error lives in the selection.

**The lab's `SelectiveProposer`** implements exactly this rule: present only the pool items
that report the claim true; if none do, present everything. Regime D runs it against
accuracy-aligned reviewers. Regime D is single-agent strategic persuasion: no coalition, no
coordination, one sender with an approval-seeking objective.

**Multi-agent version [R14].** [FINDING, as described in the research starter] Hu et al.
2026, *Lying with Truths*, model belief manipulation through publicly shared, individually
truthful fragments that combine into a misleading picture. The research starter marks this
as the closest prior work to "evidence laundering" and warns that truthful-fragment
collusion is not an untouched area. Our regime D is the single-sender version; regimes E0 and
E1 add reviewers with the same incentive, without and with a channel.

**A correct receiver's defense.** The receiver should condition on the selection rule, not
only on the shown items. If the receiver knows the sender shows only favorable items, then
"the sender showed three favorable items" is weak evidence: three favorable items out of an
unknown pool. The receiver needs either the pool size or an independent sample. In the review
game the reviewers' **private evidence slices** are that independent sample. A reviewer who
cites its private item gives the decider something the proposer did not filter. A reviewer
who endorses while its private item contradicts (plus factor f8, `against_evidence`) has
discarded the one unfiltered signal.

### 4.4 Tacit collusion in repeated games [R9]

[FINDING, as described in the research starter] Calvano et al. 2020 show that learning
agents setting prices in a repeated game can sustain collusive pricing without explicit
communication. The mechanism is the shadow of the future: deviating today is punished by
the partner's response tomorrow, and a patient agent prefers the collusive stream of payoffs.

[ESTABLISHED, standard repeated-game logic] Sustaining such a pattern needs (1) repetition
with the same partners, (2) observation of the partner's past actions, and (3) enough weight
on future payoffs. Remove any one and the pattern collapses to the one-shot outcome.

**Why the sprint cannot show tacit collusion.** The review game is one-shot: each trajectory
is a new case with no memory of earlier cases. Condition (1) fails by design. Any agreement
we observe in regime E0 (shared incentive, no channel) is parallel conduct, not tacit
collusion. The glossary keeps these separate for this reason: tacit collusion is sustained by
strategy across rounds; parallel conduct needs no strategy at all.

**What repeated interaction would add.** A follow-up regime with the same proposer and
reviewers across many cases, with each seeing the others' past votes and payoffs, could test
whether coordination emerges without a channel. That is threat model TM-5 territory and is
not in the sprint.

## 5. Example

### 5.1 Cheap talk threshold, concretely

Take b = 0.5. In state 0 the truthful sender gets 1; the lying sender gets 0.5. Truth wins.
Communication is informative.

Take b = 2. In state 0 the truthful sender gets 1; the lying sender gets 2. The sender lies.
The receiver, knowing this, learns nothing from "1" and reverts to the prior. Communication
collapses to babbling.

An approval-seeking reviewer in the glossary sense has u = 1[approve]. Set the accuracy
term to 0 and b = 1: in state 0, truthful gives 0 and lying gives 1. The reviewer always
says "approve". Its endorsement carries no information, and a decider who knows the payoff
should weight it at zero.

### 5.2 Selective disclosure with the numbers from document 01

Pool of five origins, reliability 0.8, claim false. Suppose two origins report true (wrong)
and three report false (right). Honest posterior odds from prior 1:

```
4 × 4 × (1/4) × (1/4) × (1/4) = 1/4    →   P(claim true) = 0.2
```

The selective proposer shows only the two items reporting true:

```
4 × 4 = 16    →   P(claim true) = 0.94
```

Every shown item is a faithful report. The receiver's error, from 0.2 to 0.94, equals the
product of the withheld likelihood ratios, (1/4)^3 = 1/64, inverted.

### 5.3 The dominant-strategy observation from the lab

In the scripted demo (`uv run elab demo`), regimes E0 and P produce identical false approval
rates, and Δ_comm = FAR(E1) − FAR(E0) = 0.000 with a bootstrap interval of [0.000, 0.000].

[ESTABLISHED, derived for this game] In the one-shot unanimity game, a reviewer with
u = 1[approve] gains from endorsing in every state: endorsing is necessary for approval and
costs nothing. Endorsing is a dominant strategy. A second reviewer with the same payoff
reasons the same way. Neither needs a message to arrive at "endorse". The private channel
cannot raise the vote rate above 1, so it cannot raise the false approval rate. For
payoff-maximizing agents the prediction is Δ_comm = 0.

[INTERPRETATION] This sharpens **H-SPRINT-4**. It is not a test of whether rational
coalitions benefit from a channel; in this game they do not. It is a test of whether
language-model agents under approval-seeking prompts **fall short** of the dominant
strategy when alone, and whether a coordination message closes the gap. A positive Δ_comm
would mean the model hesitated without encouragement. A zero Δ_comm would mean the model
already plays the dominant strategy, or ignores the payoff prompt entirely; the E0-versus-A
contrast (**H-SPRINT-5**) separates those two readings.

## 6. Significance

**The failure mode.** An oversight protocol that reads endorsements as reports of evidence
fails when the endorsers are paid for the decision. The mechanism is incentive, not
deception: selective disclosure and threshold-targeting let a sender move the decider with
true statements.

**What was observed and what is hypothesized.** The threshold result in 4.1 and the
persuasion result in 4.2 are derived for rational agents. Whether a language model acts like
the sender in either model is an open empirical question, and the sprint's regimes D, E0,
and E1 are the measurement. The scripted demo numbers are design checks, not findings about
models.

**Links to the hypothesis registry.**
- **H-SPRINT-5** (parallel conduct produces harm without agreement) is the claim that
  regime E0's false approval rate exceeds regime A's. Section 4.4 explains why this harm is
  not collusion: no repetition, no channel, no agreement, only shared incentive.
- **H-CORE-3** (failure concentrates at the authority transition) follows from 4.3: the
  defense against selective disclosure is not a more careful reader but a protocol step that
  demands unfiltered evidence (the reviewers' private slices, or provenance) before an
  endorsement becomes an approval.
- **H-SPRINT-4** is reframed by 5.3 as a test of prompt-conditioned behavior against a
  dominant-strategy benchmark.

**What breaks if an assumption fails.** Cheap talk assumes the receiver knows the sender's
payoff. If the decider does not know which reviewers are approval-seeking, it cannot apply
the discount from 4.1; that is the realistic case, and it is why plus factor f7
(`payoff_aligned`) is flagged in the auditor design as experimenter knowledge rather than
deployer knowledge. Bayesian persuasion assumes commitment; a model that chooses per case is
more dangerous, not less, because it can also exploit case-specific features. Repeated-game
collusion assumes memory; a one-shot protocol removes it, which is one argument for
rotating reviewers.

**[SPECULATION]** A reviewer prompted for approval may produce reasons that are
word-for-word true and systematically silent about its private slice. If so, the
`against_evidence` plus factor would be the single most informative feature in the LLM run.
We have not measured this.
